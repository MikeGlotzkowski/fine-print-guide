Renting a home

When you rent, the landlord insures the building. Your belongings, your hotel if the place becomes unlivable, and your liability are up to you.

At a glance

  1. Read what your lease requires Often required by someone else
  2. Buy a renters policy Optional
  3. Record what you own Optional
  4. Check flood risk if you're on a ground floor Optional

  1. 1 Read what your lease requires

    Often required by someone else

    Many leases require renters insurance with a minimum liability limit, often $100,000, and ask you to name the landlord as an "interested party" so they're told if you cancel.

  2. 2 Buy a renters policy

    Optional

    Pick a personal property limit that would replace everything you own. Replacement cost costs a little more; actual cash value pays little for used furniture.

    Read more: Renters insurance

  3. 3 Record what you own

    Optional

    Walk through each room with your phone camera, opening closets and drawers. Store the video online. It makes a claim much faster.

  4. 4 Check flood risk if you're on a ground floor

    Optional

    Renters policies exclude flood. You can buy contents-only flood insurance, often cheaply outside high-risk zones.

    Read more: Flood insurance

Things that often surprise people

  • Your roommate's things aren't covered by your policy unless they're named on it.
  • If you cause a fire or a leak, the landlord's insurer can pay the landlord and then come after you through subrogation.
  • Things stolen from your car are covered by renters insurance, not car insurance.

General information, not advice. Policies differ by insurer and by state. What your own policy says is what counts, so use this site to know what to look for and what to ask.
